Media-Frames Checklists

A "frame" is the way a story is angled so it pushes you toward one feeling or conclusion. Fake posts rarely fake every fact — they fake the frame around real-ish facts. Each checklist below uses the same five questions with topic-specific traps to watch for.

The five questions (works for any topic)

  1. Who benefits if I share this? Follow the like, the donations link, the shop, or the subscription in the bio.
  2. What is the original source? A named agency, study, or footage with a date — not "doctors are saying."
  3. What emotion is this made to trigger? Fear, urgency, outrage, or "share before they delete it" are the usual levers.
  4. What is missing? Numbers without a date, quotes without a name, images without a location.
  5. What would change my mind? If nothing could, it is not a news item; it is a persuasion piece.

Health rumor checklist

  • ☐ Does the post claim a new cure, warning, or recall that the CDC or a hospital's own site does not mention?
  • ☐ Does it attack mainstream medicine as a whole ("they won't tell you this") instead of citing a specific study?
  • ☐ Does it ask you to boost a product — vitamins, essential-oil blends, supplements, cleanses — by name?
  • ☐ Is the "study" real? Search the study name or researcher at the journal's own site, not just the post.
  • ☐ Are the scary images actually old? Run them through the reverse-image routes in tools.md.
  • ☐ Does it mix a true general fact with a false specific claim ("it is true the flu mutates — so this supplement stops it")?
  • ☐ If it is about a health agency, open the agency's own site (https://www.cdc.gov) and search for the topic there.

Remember: none of this is medical advice. It is how to check whether the post is trustworthy before you forward it.

Money rumor checklist

  • ☐ Claimed windfalls — refunds, stimulus checks, class payouts, lawsuit settlements — should appear on the paying agency's own site (start at https://www.irs.gov for anything tax-related).
  • ☐ Bank, payment, or delivery "alerts" that arrive by text: do not tap the link in the post or message; open the company's site yourself or type its address you already know.
  • ☐ Crypto and resale "opportunities" that show screenshots of huge profits but no verifiable history; ask for the dated ledger, not the screenshot.
  • ☐ "Send this to ten friends and get" — nothing official ever works that way.
  • ☐ Too-precise statistics ("92.4 million people lost money") with no source or date? Check the number against Census Bureau or Bureau of Labor Statistics data before repeating it.
  • ☐ Does the post make you feel behind — rich people know this, everyone else missed it — then offer exactly one way out?

Weather rumor checklist

  • ☐ Does the image or map claim to be current? Weather screenshots and radar images are recycled constantly; search the image first (see tools.md).
  • ☐ Named hurricane or storm forecasts should be readable on the National Hurricane Center's own page: https://www.nhc.noaa.gov
  • ☐ Severe-planning terms — watches, warnings, radar — come from the National Weather Service: https://www.weather.gov
  • ☐ Circular "storm shadow" radar art and perfectly hexagonal hurricane eyes are classic fakes; real radar has messy edges.
  • ☐ Dates on the graphic: a real forecast names its issue time; undated "incoming!" graphics are almost always old news.
  • ☐ Local emergency-management pages (county or city) beat any forwarded screenshot for actual instructions.

One-line version to remember

Source, date, older-or-newer image, who profits, official site. Five checks, under two minutes, before you forward.
