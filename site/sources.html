Fact-Checking Sources

Free places to check whether a claim, image, or statistic is real. Every entry below links to an official site. Nothing here costs money at the tier you need, and there are no affiliate links anywhere.

Professional fact-check sites

Snopes

  • Link: https://www.snopes.com
  • What it is: One of the oldest fact-checking sites, covering viral rumors, images, memes, and "did they really say that" claims.
  • Use it for: Urban legends, celebrity quotes, scam screenshots, and anything forwarded with "is this real?"
  • Free tier: Articles are free to read; an optional paid tier (Snopes+) removes ads but is not required for anything here.

PolitiFact

  • Link: https://www.politifact.com
  • What it is: Truth-O-Meter ratings from fact-checkers who trace claims back to original sources.
  • Use it for: Statements by politicians, campaigns, and viral political posts; use their site search before you believe a quote.
  • Free tier: Entirely free to read, with an optional newsletter.

AP Fact Checks

  • Link: https://www.apnews.com/hub/fact-checking
  • What it is: The Associated Press's fact-checking hub, including its recurring "Not Real News" roundups.
  • Use it for: Doctored photos, fabricated quotes attributed to public figures, and items from the weekly wrong-news roundups.
  • Free tier: Free to read.

Reuters Fact Check

  • Link: https://www.reuters.com/fact-check/
  • What it is: Reuters's fact-check desk, strong on viral video, manipulated images, and miscaptioned footage.
  • Use it for: "Look at this video" claims, old footage posted as new, and AI-generated image debunks.
  • Free tier: Free with a simple registration after a few visits.

FactCheck.org

  • Link: https://www.factcheck.org
  • What it is: A nonpartisan fact-checker run by the Annenberg Public Policy Center at the University of Pennsylvania.
  • Use it for: Political ads, viral claims about laws and policies, and the Scam Check / Viral Spiral features.
  • Free tier: Entirely free to read.

Official data sources

CDC (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention)

  • Link: https://www.cdc.gov
  • What it is: The official U.S. public-health agency site, with data, guidance, and outbreak updates.
  • Use it for: Confirming or deflating health claims going around your group chat — "the CDC just confirmed..." posts especially.
  • Free tier: Entirely free, no account needed.
  • Tip: Check the page date. Health guidance is often updated, and old screenshots get recirculated as if they were new.

U.S. Census Bureau

  • Link: https://www.census.gov
  • What it is: Official U.S. population and demographic data, including QuickFacts for states, counties, and cities.
  • Use it for: Viral "the population of X is actually..." claims and chain-message statistics about towns, states, or schools.
  • Free tier: Entirely free, no account needed.

IRS (Internal Revenue Service)

  • Link: https://www.irs.gov
  • What it is: The official U.S. tax agency, including newsroom alerts on current tax scams.
  • Use it for: Checking "the IRS announced a new refund/contact stimulus" messages before anyone clicks a link in one.
  • Free tier: Entirely free, no account needed to read warnings.
  • Tip: The IRS scams-and-alerts page (https://www.irs.gov/help/tax-scams-in-consumer-alerts) explains how the IRS really reaches people — almost never by text or social-media message.

How to check a quote or claim quickly

  1. Copy the most surprising sentence of the post.
  2. Search it in quotes on Snopes, PolitiFact, FactCheck.org, and AP/Reuters, one at a time.
  3. If it involves a number, look for the number on the official source's own site (CDC, Census, IRS).
  4. If you find nothing either way, say "I could not verify this" instead of forwarding it as true.
